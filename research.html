Identifying Bank-Level Financial Shocks: A Theory-Informed Structural Panel VAR

Paper (PDF)

Banks transmit and shape macroeconomic fluctuations. However, observable changes in bank balance sheets do not reveal whether the underlying disturbances originate from borrowers, depositors, or banks themselves. This paper develops a theory-informed method to disentangle these forces using widely available regulatory data. A dynamic banking model generates restrictions on the joint responses of lending, loan returns, and deposit returns to loan-demand, deposit-supply, and adverse internal bank-cost shocks. I apply the method to U.S. quarterly Call Reports covering 1983–2026 and recover bank-specific shock histories. Positive loan-demand and deposit-supply shocks are associated with higher next-quarter lending, deposits, and employment, while adverse cost shocks are associated with contraction. Low-capital banks exhibit larger deposit contractions following weak loan demand. The framework provides a tool for distinguishing the shocks banks face and studying how they adjust their portfolios.

Age-Dependent Financing: Asset- vs. Earnings-Based Credits in Firms

While canonical models assume firms borrow against assets, recent evidence highlights the importance of borrowing against earnings. Using linked Compustat–DealScan data, I find tentative evidence that older firms are more likely to use earnings-based credit, while younger firms tend to use asset-based credit. This paper explains the age pattern through two mechanisms: older firms have less uncertain earnings, allowing them to borrow more against earnings, and low-profit firms exit early, improving the survivor profitability. A simulated economy with entry and exit reproduces this pattern when lenders account for costly recovery following earnings shortfalls.

The Impact of China’s Universal Two-Child Policy on Female Labor Market Outcomes

Paper (PDF)

At the end of 2015, the Chinese government ended the decades-long one-child policy in response to the unsustainably low fertility rate. Beginning in 2016, all couples were allowed to have two children instead of one. This paper examines the impact of China’s universal two-child policy on the labor market outcomes of women of childbearing age. I begin with a difference-in-differences analysis to assess the policy’s overall effects. I then examine variation in fertility responses across groups defined by Chinese Communist Party (CCP) membership, provincial fertility-policy strictness, and urban versus rural residence. I use this heterogeneity in a triple-difference analysis of female labor market outcomes. My findings are twofold. First, fertility rates generally increased after the one-child policy was relaxed, with female CCP members experiencing the largest increase. Second, the two-child policy primarily affected female CCP members’ labor market outcomes, reducing employment and labor force participation within this group.

The Predictive Power of the Term Structure Under Unconventional Monetary Policies

Honors thesis

Historically, a flattening or inversion of the yield curve has often preceded economic slowdowns, making the yield spread a valuable indicator of future economic activity. However, its predictive power may change when central banks directly influence the term structure of interest rates through unconventional monetary policies. Following the Global Financial Crisis, large-scale asset purchases and negative interest rates raised questions about whether the yield spread continues to convey reliable information about future economic growth. This paper examines how the relationship between the yield spread and future real GDP growth varies across monetary policy regimes. Using quarterly data from eight advanced economies, I estimate predictive regressions across horizons ranging from one quarter to five years. I also investigate whether the adoption and magnitude of unconventional monetary policies are associated with changes in this relationship. The results suggest that the yield spread's explanatory power generally declines when the sample is extended to include unconventional monetary policy periods, although the patterns vary substantially across countries. These findings highlight the importance of considering monetary policy regimes when interpreting the yield curve as a signal of future economic activity.
